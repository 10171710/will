Providing for a child who will always need help

Why leaving a direct lump-sum bequest in a will is rarely kind to a vulnerable dependant, and how a private special needs trust safeguards their lifetime dignity.

Devan Nair Partner · Trusts & Settlements · STEP Affiliate
Share

For parents of a child with an intellectual impairment, severe autism, cerebral palsy, or chronic psychiatric illness, no fear cuts deeper than: "What happens to our child when we are no longer here to care for them?"

The instinctive reaction of loving parents is often to write in their Will: "I leave all my fixed deposits and our home to my daughter."

While this intention is pure, a direct bequest often creates immediate perils for a vulnerable adult child. In this guide, we examine why direct bequests fail and how to construct a robust Special Needs Private Trust under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882.

The Three Fatal Flaws of a Direct Will Bequest

Inability to Operate

If the individual lacks contractual legal capacity, banks will freeze the funds until someone obtains a cumbersome guardianship certificate under the National Trust Act or Mental Healthcare Act.

Predatory Exploitation

A vulnerable person with substantial assets in their name is an easy target for distant relatives, unscrupulous acquaintances, or dubious schemes.

No Care Guidance

Money alone cannot hire quality speech therapists, ensure regular physiotherapy, or maintain dietary preferences without an active managing structure.

A direct lump-sum bequest places a target on a vulnerable child's back. A private trust surrounds them with a shield, an engine, and a steady hand.

The Architecture of a Special Needs Trust

A private trust is a legal arrangement where assets are transferred to a board of trustees to be held and managed solely for the benefit of your child (the sole beneficiary):

  • The Settlor(s): Usually the parents during their lifetime, or established via a testamentary trust clause in their Will.
  • The Board of Trustees: A balanced committee comprising at least two loving relatives/siblings combined with an institutional or professional co-trustee (to ensure continuity if relatives move abroad or age).
  • The Beneficiary: The child, who receives periodic income, medical coverage, assisted living funding, and recreational allowances.
  • The Trust Protector / Advisory Board: An independent doctor, family friend, or NGO representative empowered to inspect living conditions and remove negligent trustees.

The "Letter of Wishes" (Care Protocol)

While the Trust Deed is the formal legal instrument, the Letter of Wishes is the human soul of the plan. It contains intimate instructions that no court statute could ever formulate:

Key Elements to Document in Your Care Protocol:

  • Medical history & medications: Doctors, preferred hospitals, allergy notes, dosage schedules.
  • Daily routines & sensory triggers: Calming techniques, food preferences, sensory aversions.
  • Caregiver standards: Ideal caregiver-to-patient ratio and minimum respite days.
  • Living arrangements: Whether the child remains in the family home with 24/7 staff or transitions to a specialized assisted living facility.

How to Fund the Trust Corpus

A trust can be created with a nominal initial amount (e.g. ₹10,000) during your lifetime and then fully funded upon your demise through:

  • Term Life Insurance / Whole Life Policies: The trust is named as the registered policy beneficiary or assigned under the Insurance Act.
  • Rental Income from Commercial/Residential Property: Real estate is settled into the trust to generate predictable monthly rents.
  • Testamentary Bequest in the Parents' Will: The Will directs all residual liquid investments directly into the trust bank account.

The Remainder / Residual Clause

What happens to the trust capital after the child eventually passes away? The trust deed specifies a residual clause. You can direct the remaining funds to other children, grandchildren, or a charitable foundation supporting other special needs families.
