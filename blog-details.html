Who can witness your will — and who absolutely cannot

It is the shortest rule in succession law and the one that quietly ruins the most home-made wills. Two minutes of attention at the signing table saves a family years.

Anita Willbridge Founder & Principal · 26 years in succession
Share

Almost every week, someone brings us a will written by a parent or a grandparent, signed properly, dated properly, kept safely — and partially void. Not because the wishes were unclear, but because the person who witnessed it stood to inherit under it.

The rule, in one sentence

A will must be signed by the person making it in the presence of two witnesses, who each then sign in that person's presence — and a witness (or a witness's spouse) who is also a beneficiary risks losing the gift made to them.

The will itself usually survives. The gift to that witness is what fails. So the very person you most wanted to provide for can be the one person who receives nothing, purely because they were the helpful one who signed at the bottom of the page.

The document is not what fails. The kindness in it is.

Who should never witness

Any beneficiary

Children, siblings, partners or friends who are receiving anything under the will.

Spouse of a beneficiary

Even if the spouse receives nothing directly, their signature can void their partner's gift.

Who makes a good witness

Neighbours, independent friends, colleagues, or professionals (doctors, lawyers, chartered accountants) who have no financial interest in your estate whatsoever.

An executor may witness — an executor is not automatically a beneficiary — but if your executor is also inheriting, keep them away from the witness line entirely.

A short signing checklist

  1. Everyone in the same room, at the same time, before anybody signs.
  2. You sign first, in ink, on every page.
  3. Both witnesses watch you do it — actually watch, not glance up afterwards.
  4. Each witness signs, with full name, address and the date.
  5. Nobody leaves the room until all three signatures are on the page.
  6. Note down who witnessed it and where they can be found.

What if it has already gone wrong?

Do not amend the original — crossing out and re-signing creates a fresh set of problems. The clean fix is a new will that expressly revokes the old one, signed correctly this time. It usually takes one appointment, and it is far cheaper than arguing about the first one after a death.

If the person who made the will has already died, the position is more delicate but not hopeless. Courts can admit a will to probate despite an interested witness in certain circumstances, and there are established routes to prove due execution. That is a conversation to have quickly rather than eventually.

Why we witness in the office

Every will we draft is signed at our office with two independent witnesses from our own staff, a short capacity note on the file, and a record of who was present. It costs us twenty minutes. It removes the single most common ground for a challenge.
