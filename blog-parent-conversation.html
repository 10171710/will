How to raise the subject with your parents

Five gentle openings that do not sound like you are counting the silver. Practical, respectful ways adult children can begin estate and care discussions without friction.

Tanvi Deshmukh Practice Manager & Client Care · Family Facilitator
Share

Almost every adult child we speak to has the same apprehension: "I want to make sure my parents' affairs are in order, but I am terrified they will think I am asking for their money."

As a result, most families put off the conversation until a medical emergency forces it upon them in a hospital corridor. That is the worst time to discover that bank accounts lack nominations, locker keys cannot be found, and nobody knows who holds the power of attorney.

Here are five respectful, field-tested conversational openings that focus entirely on your parents' peace of mind, health wishes, and administrative clarity.

1. The "Third-Party Friction" Opening

Instead of making the discussion about your family out of nowhere, anchor it in an external event — a colleague's recent probate ordeal, a news story about frozen bank accounts, or an estate planning guide you recently read.

"A friend at work just spent eight months running between court registries because his father passed away without written bank nominations. It made me realize how much unnecessary stress families go through. Have you and Mum ever looked at whether all your account records are updated?"

This places the blame on bureaucratic inefficiency rather than mortality, creating an invitation to collaborate.

2. The "Lead by Example" Opening

The most disarming way to discuss wills is to write your own first. When you openly tell your parents that you have drafted your own will, named guardians for your children, and created an asset inventory, it normalizes succession planning as basic adult hygiene.

"I finally met with an estate lawyer this week to make my own will and record all my digital passwords and insurance policies. Going through the exercise made me realize how much paperwork is involved. Could you look over how I organized it and tell me if I missed anything from your experience?"

Framing succession as responsible administration rather than an end-of-life ritual strips away the emotional discomfort.

3. The Healthcare and Medical Proxy Focus

Many aging parents are reluctant to talk about property distribution, but very eager to talk about medical dignity. They care deeply about who will speak for them if they are incapacitated, whether they want artificial life support, and where they wish to receive care.

Starting with a Living Will (Advance Medical Directive) and Healthcare Power of Attorney opens the door naturally. Once healthcare wishes are settled, transitioning to property distribution feels like a logical next step.

4. The "Where Is the Red Folder?" Logistics Opening

You do not need to know what is in their will or who gets what asset. What you do need is the location of essential documents in an emergency.

  • Where are physical title deeds and share certificates kept?
  • Which bank branch holds the primary safe deposit locker, and who is registered on the joint mandate?
  • Who is their trusted chartered accountant or family lawyer?
  • Where is their medical insurance card and policy schedule?

"You don't need to share numbers or details with me. I just want to make sure that if either of you is unwell, I know exactly which doctor to call, which drawer has the health cards, and where the locker key is kept so I can support you properly."

5. The "Protecting Your Legacy & Wishes" Opening

When there are multiple siblings or extended family members, a parent's greatest unstated fear is family disharmony after they are gone. Reassure them that a written, legally binding will is the greatest gift of harmony they can leave behind.

"We want whatever you decide to be respected down to the last letter. If things are written down clearly in a formal will, nobody in the family will ever have to guess, debate, or disagree about what you intended."

Essential Ground Rules

Do

  • Pick a calm Sunday afternoon at home, not a festival or busy holiday.
  • Have one-on-one conversations first before gathering all siblings.
  • Offer to connect them with an independent legal advisor where you are not present in the room.

Don't

  • Don't bring up the topic during a heated family argument or immediately after a bereavement.
  • Don't demand to know the monetary value of their assets or their division percentages.
  • Don't rush them to make instant decisions; give them time to reflect.
