When a family trust is worth it — and when it is not

Trusts are heavily oversold by wealth managers. Here are the four specific situations where a private family trust genuinely earns its keep under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882.

Devan Nair Partner · Trusts & Settlements · STEP affiliate
Share

At least twice a month, a client comes to our office asking to create a private family trust because their private banker told them it was the "modern" thing to do. We usually talk them out of it. A well-drafted will costs a tenth of the price, requires zero annual tax filings during your lifetime, and achieves 95% of what a typical family actually needs.

However, there are four specific circumstances where a private family trust under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 is not merely useful — it is the only vehicle that will work.

1. Providing for a Special Needs or Vulnerable Dependant

If you have a child with an intellectual disability, autism, or a severe physical condition, leaving them a direct bequest in a will creates two grave risks: they cannot legally manage bank accounts without a court-appointed guardian, and predatory relatives or third parties can siphon the funds.

A Special Needs Private Trust holds the capital safely. You appoint a blend of trusted family members and an institutional or professional co-trustee. The trust deed dictates exact monthly maintenance disbursements, covers therapy, caregiver salaries, medical emergencies, and specifies what happens to the remaining corpus after the child's lifetime.

A lump-sum bequest leaves a vulnerable child with a target on their back. A trust gives them a shield and a steady hand.

2. Blended Families and Second Marriages

When you remarry and have children from a previous marriage, standard wills create intense anxiety:

  • If you leave everything to your new spouse, they might later change their will and exclude your children.
  • If you leave everything directly to your children, your surviving spouse could be left without a home or adequate income.

A trust resolves this neatly through a Life Interest clause: the surviving spouse receives all rental income, dividends, and the right to reside in the family home for their lifetime, but the underlying capital is permanently locked for your biological children.

3. Ring-Fencing Assets from Business and Creditor Risk

Entrepreneurs and promoters who provide personal guarantees for corporate debt risk their entire family estate if a business venture fails. An Irrevocable Discretionary Trust created when you are completely solvent and free of debt claims moves asset ownership out of your personal name. Under Indian law, creditors cannot attach trust assets for debts incurred years after the settlement, provided there was no fraudulent intent at the time of creation.

4. Staggered Inheritance for Young Beneficiaries

Direct inheritance vests completely at age 18. Handing millions of rupees in liquid assets to an eighteen-year-old frequently ends in hasty investments and poor decisions.

A trust allows you to stagger milestones: for example, 25% at age 21 for postgraduate tuition, 25% at age 25 for a first home down-payment, and the remaining 50% at age 30 when career and financial habits have matured.

When you do NOT need a trust

Standard Two-Child Estates

If your children are healthy adults and your assets are straightforward real estate and mutual funds, a mirror will is all you need.

Avoiding Estate Duty Myths

India abolished estate duty in 1985. Setting up a trust purely to avoid inheritance tax that does not currently exist is an unnecessary expense.
