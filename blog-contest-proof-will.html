Six things that make a will harder to contest

Small drafting and procedural decisions that quietly dismantle grounds for future caveats and legal challenges before they can ever reach a courtroom.

Anita Willbridge Founder & Principal Advocate · 26 years in succession
Share

No lawyer can ethically tell you that a Will can be made 100% immune from someone filing a frivolous caveat. Anyone with a court fee stamp can attempt to file a petition.

However, what we can do is make the Will so robustly documented and executed that any frivolous challenge collapses at the preliminary hearing.

In Indian law, challenges to wills almost always rely on four allegations: lack of testamentary capacity, undue influence/coercion, suspicious circumstances surrounding signing, or improper witnessing.

Here are the six practical steps that neutralize these allegations completely.

1. The Doctor as an Attesting Witness

The most common challenge from a disinherited relative is: "The testator was elderly, suffering from dementia, and did not understand what they were signing."

To eliminate this, have your registered family physician or a qualified geriatrician examine you on the signing day and sign as one of the two formal attesting witnesses (or attach a contemporaneous Certificate of Mental Fitness). When a licensed doctor testifies to sound disposing mind, the "lack of capacity" argument is dead on arrival.

2. Video-Recording the Execution Ceremony

Under modern evidentiary standards, courts give immense weight to timestamped, continuous video footage of the will execution.

What the Video Must Capture:

  • • The testator stating today's date, their name, and confirming they are acting freely.
  • • The testator reading out the summary of the key bequests.
  • • The testator signing every page, followed immediately by both witnesses signing in their full view.
A judge who can watch the testator calmly articulate their wishes on video will rarely entertain allegations of fraud or duress.

3. Keeping Beneficiaries Out of the Signing Room

A frequent ground for "suspicious circumstances" is the active participation of the major beneficiary during drafting or signing. If Son A drove his father to the lawyer's office, sat beside him during drafting, and held the pen, courts view this with intense suspicion.

Ensure that any child or relative receiving a major share is strictly absent from both the consultation room and the execution table.

4. Explicit, Respectful Rationales for Unequal Distributions

If you are leaving unequal shares to your children — for instance, leaving a house to your daughter and none to your son — never simply omit the son's name without explanation. Silence invites allegations that you "forgot" him or were manipulated.

Include a dignified, matter-of-fact clause:

"I have made no bequest in this Will to my son Rahul, not out of any lack of affection, but because I have already provided him with substantial financial capital during my lifetime for his business in 2021."

5. Voluntary Registration with the Sub-Registrar

While registration of a Will is optional under Section 18 of the Registration Act, 1908, voluntary registration creates a powerful legal presumption of genuine execution. The Sub-Registrar verifies the testator's identity, biometrics, and photographs, making claims of forgery virtually impossible to sustain.

6. Express Revocation and Alignment of Nominations

Old handwritten notes, partial codicils, or outdated bank nominations create massive confusion. State unequivocally in clause 1: "I hereby revoke all previous Wills and testamentary dispositions made by me at any time." Then, update all bank and mutual fund nominations to match.
