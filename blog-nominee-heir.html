A nominee is not an heir. Here is why that matters

The single largest source of post-death litigation in Indian families is the mistaken belief that naming someone as bank or demat nominee makes them the rightful owner.

Anita Willbridge Founder & Principal · 26 years in succession
Share

If there is one phrase I have had to repeat thousands of times over 26 years in succession practice, it is this: a nomination is a convenience for the bank, not a bequest for your heir.

Countless people believe that because they nominated their eldest child on their fixed deposits, savings accounts, or mutual funds, that money automatically belongs to that child after their death. In law, this is completely untrue.

What a Nominee Actually Is in Law

Under settled Supreme Court jurisprudence (dating back to Smt. Sarojini Amma and reiterated in Ram Chander Talwar v. Devender Kumar Talwar), a nominee is merely a trustee / custodian.

When the account holder dies, the bank or financial institution pays out the funds to the nominee to give the bank a valid legal discharge. However, the nominee does not become the owner. The nominee holds the money in trust and is legally obligated to hand it over to the legal heirs entitled under the deceased's Will (or under the laws of intestate succession if there is no will).

The nominee is the hand that receives the money from the bank; the heir is the pocket in which it must legally stay.

How Nomination Works Across Different Assets

Asset Category Role of Nominee Who Ultimately Owns It?
Bank Accounts & FDs Trustee / Custodian Beneficiaries named in the Will (or Class I legal heirs)
Mutual Funds & Demat Shares Fiduciary Custodian Testamentary heirs under Will override nomination (Supreme Court Shakti Yezdani ruling)
Life Insurance Policies Beneficial Nominee* If immediate family (spouse, child, parent), nominee holds full beneficial ownership under Sec 39
Co-operative Housing Society Flat Administrative Trustee Society transfers share certificate to nominee for admin; title remains with legal heirs under Will

The Classic Family Dispute Scenario

Consider a common situation: A father has two children, Son A (living with him) and Daughter B (living in another city). The father names Son A as the nominee on all bank accounts so Son A can manage daily expenses and medical emergencies.

In his Will, the father writes: "All my bank deposits and financial assets shall be divided equally 50/50 between my son and daughter."

After the father passes, the bank transfers 100% of the ₹1.5 crore balance to Son A because he is the registered nominee. Son A assumes the money is his gift and refuses to give Daughter B her half.

Daughter B is forced to file a partition suit in court. After three years of litigation, the court orders Son A to pay Daughter B her 50% share plus interest and legal costs. The family bond is shattered forever over a misunderstanding of nomination laws.

The 3-Step Clean-Up Protocol

1

Audit Your Existing Nominations

Pull out records for all bank accounts, mutual funds, provident funds (EPF/PPF), and insurance policies. Many nominations were set up decades ago and still name deceased relatives or ex-spouses.

2

Align Nominations with Your Will

Ensure that whenever possible, your registered nominees match the exact beneficiaries designated in your Will for that specific asset.

3

Add an Express Clause in Your Will

Explicitly state in your Will that any nomination filed with any bank or financial institution is purely for administrative discharge and shall be subject to the distribution terms of your Will.

Summary

Never rely on nomination forms as a substitute for an estate plan. A formal, well-drafted Will remains the only instrument that transfers genuine legal title and protects your heirs from bitter courtroom battles.
